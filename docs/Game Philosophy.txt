# PUCA — DESIGN THEORY AND GAMEPLAY PHILOSOPHY

## What Puca is

Puca is a natural-language role-playing game where the player types what they want their character to do.

There are no conventional action menus, dialogue trees, visible RPG stats, or lists of available interactions. The player simply describes their intention in ordinary language.

The core loop is:

**THE GAME DESCRIBES THE WORLD
→ THE PLAYER TYPES WHAT THEY WANT TO DO
→ AN LLM INTERPRETS WHAT THEY MEAN
→ PYTHON DECIDES WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS
→ THE WORLD CHANGES
→ AN LLM DESCRIBES THAT NEW REALITY BACK TO THE PLAYER**

The central rule is:

> **The LLM understands and communicates. Python owns reality.**

---

## Wide language, bounded reality

The player should be able to express almost any sensible intention.

They might:

* inspect something;
* manipulate an object;
* attack someone;
* lie;
* refuse;
* run away;
* wait;
* sing;
* hide;
* attempt something ridiculous;
* combine several actions together.

The game should try to understand the meaning behind that language rather than force the player to discover specific commands.

However, understanding an action does not mean allowing it.

The world remains physically, socially and mechanically constrained.

The desired experience is:

> **“The game understood what I wanted to do, but reality stopped me.”**

Never:

> **“The game rejected my sentence because I didn't use the right words.”**

---

## The player controls intention, not guaranteed action

A major part of Puca is the distinction between:

**what the player wants the character to do;**

**what the character actually manages to do;**

and

**what the world allows to happen.**

The player owns intention.

Python determines execution.

A frightened, exhausted, injured, inexperienced or socially overwhelmed character may fail to perform exactly what the player requested.

A player might type:

> `Punch him.`

But the character may freeze.

They may hesitate.

They may attempt it weakly.

In extreme cases, they may do something almost opposite to what the player wanted.

The narrator should acknowledge this conflict directly.

This makes failure, embarrassment, fear and bodily limitation part of the game rather than invisible restrictions imposed by the engine.

---

## Python is the authoritative world

Python owns all facts that matter.

This includes:

* locations;
* objects;
* NPCs;
* injuries;
* physical capability;
* inventory;
* doors and locks;
* time;
* world events;
* NPC goals and behaviour;
* hunger;
* thirst;
* fatigue;
* cleanliness;
* relationships;
* knowledge;
* language ability;
* hazards;
* combat;
* Luck;
* persistent consequences.

The LLM cannot invent these things.

If Python says an object does not exist, the narrator cannot introduce it.

If Python says an NPC is unconscious, that NPC cannot continue acting.

If the player leaves an object behind, it stays there until something in the world moves it.

The world should remember what happens.

---

## The LLM works in both directions

The LLM is fundamental to Puca, but it does not control the game world.

It has two major jobs.

### Understanding the player

The interpretation LLM converts natural language into structured intent.

It works out what the player actually means, including:

* unusual phrasing;
* pronouns;
* slang;
* mistakes;
* compound actions;
* emotional tone;
* indirect instructions;
* follow-up references.

It does not decide whether the action succeeds.

### Representing the world

The narration LLM receives authoritative facts from Python and turns them into natural prose.

Python decides:

> what happened.

The narrator decides:

> how the player experiences what happened.

This is what allows Puca to have flexible, expressive prose without surrendering control of reality to generative AI.

---

## The narrator

The narrator speaks directly to the player in the second person.

It should feel intimate, observant, occasionally dry, occasionally absurd, and highly aware of the difference between intention and reality.

It can describe:

* bodily sensations;
* hesitation;
* fear;
* memory;
* instinct;
* embarrassment;
* perception;
* reasonable inference.

It does not know things the character cannot know.

It should never expose game mechanics.

The player should not see:

* HP;
* STAMINA;
* SKILL;
* LUCK;
* dice rolls;
* DCs;
* damage numbers;
* passage numbers;
* combat rounds;
* hidden state.

Instead of:

> `You lose 3 HP.`

the player experiences:

> Teeth close around your calf. When the animal tears free, your leg gives way beneath you.

The mechanics remain real.

Only their representation changes.

---

## Failure is content

Puca should not treat failure as a dead end.

A failed action should often produce some of the most interesting narration and consequences.

A player who tries something reckless may:

* hurt themselves;
* attract attention;
* lose an object;
* anger an NPC;
* become exhausted;
* trigger a hazard;
* expose themselves to an enemy.

The world does not pause because the player's action was foolish.

If the player sings while something is trying to kill them, the creature does not politely wait for the song to finish.

The player sings.

Time passes.

The creature attacks.

The important rule is:

> **Bad judgement should increase exposure to real dangers already present in the situation.**

Not:

> **Invalid input causes random punishment.**

---

## The world has agency too

Puca is not made from static rooms waiting for the player to choose something.

NPCs and environments have their own behaviour.

NPCs have:

* goals;
* schedules;
* knowledge;
* fears;
* duties;
* relationships;
* locations.

Events can happen because time passes.

People can arrive, leave, attack, intervene or change plans without waiting for the player to trigger the correct dialogue option.

The player is an actor inside the world.

They are not the mechanism that makes the world exist.

---

## Hidden bodily state

The character's body is an important part of gameplay.

Python may track things such as:

* hunger;
* thirst;
* fatigue;
* cleanliness;
* comfort;
* pain;
* injury.

These are never shown as meters.

Instead they change what becomes important in the narration.

Hunger makes food harder to ignore.

Fatigue makes a bed increasingly attractive.

Pain changes how movement feels.

Dirty skin makes washing more desirable.

The player should experience bodily state rather than manage a dashboard.

---

## Authored depth and systemic breadth

Puca combines two kinds of interaction.

### Systemic breadth

General rules allow ordinary actions such as:

* move;
* take;
* drop;
* push;
* pull;
* open;
* close;
* throw;
* attack;
* hide;
* inspect;
* speak;
* wait.

These should work across many different situations.

### Authored depth

Important situations can contain specially authored possibilities and consequences.

The player should not see these as choices.

They should discover them naturally through interaction.

The goal is not to predict every sentence the player might type.

The goal is to build situations where a wide range of likely human behaviour produces an intelligible response.

---

## Persistent consequences

The world should remember.

If the player:

* moves something;
* injures someone;
* breaks something;
* hides an object;
* spills food;
* learns information;
* frightens an NPC;
* makes an enemy;

those facts should continue to matter.

Leaving and returning should not reset the scene.

Saving and loading should preserve the same world.

Persistence is what turns free-text interaction from improvisation into gameplay.

---

## Variable worlds and replayability

Where appropriate, locations such as dungeons can use seeded procedural layouts.

The encounters themselves may remain authored and learnable, while their order, connections, routes, shortcuts and dependencies vary between runs.

This creates a useful balance:

> **Players can learn encounters without memorising the entire journey.**

Repeated play should feel familiar without becoming identical.

---

## The overall design principle

Puca should not attempt to simulate an infinite world.

Instead it should create strong, legible situations in which a very wide range of player intentions can be meaningfully understood and resolved.

The design philosophy can be summarised as:

> **Wide expression.
> Bounded reality.
> Deep consequences.
> Persistent worlds.
> Python-owned truth.
> LLM-mediated understanding and narration.**

And the most important player-facing promise is:

> **You can try almost anything.
> The game will understand what you mean.
> But the world is under no obligation to let you get away with it.**
